## Facts and sources
1. Carbon monoxide has no colour, smell or taste. Miners feared it most after fires and explosions ("afterdamp").
2. John Scott Haldane proposed carrying small animals, mainly canaries, as early CO detectors. Canaries breathe fast and their lungs pull air through in one direction on both breaths, so CO reaches their blood long before a miner feels anything. A bird swaying or dropping off its perch meant get out.
3. British pits kept canaries until about 1986, when electronic detectors replaced them.
4. **Payoff fact:** a "canary resuscitator" cage made by Siebe Gorman (from 1896) had a small oxygen cylinder on top. When the bird collapsed, the door was shut and the valve opened to revive it.
   - Science Museum Group, *Cage for reviving canary*: https://collection.sciencemuseumgroup.org.uk/objects/co8412250/cage-for-reviving-canary
   - Amgueddfa Cymru / Big Pit National Coal Museum, *Haldane's humane canary cage*: https://museum.wales/collections/online/object/1868c85f-8e50-3a67-93b6-168b7a036428
   - Science and Industry Museum (Manchester) blog, *Canary resuscitator*: https://blog.scienceandindustrymuseum.org.uk/canary-resuscitator
   - Caveat for the last line: ordinary miners on a shift carried no oxygen. Rescue teams did, from about 1907. So the script says **"on the shift"**, not "in the mine".
